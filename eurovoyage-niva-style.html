EuroVoyage — a decision-first transit experience

The problem isn't finding the train. It's knowing you're doing it right. A decision-first transit experience for travelers navigating unfamiliar European cities.

Problem

Finding a route is easy. Knowing how to use the transit system is not.

When travelers arrive in an unfamiliar city, they often already know where they want to go. What they don't know is how the local transit system works.

Different cities use different ticket types, fare zones, validation rules, terminology, payment systems, and transfer rules. For someone unfamiliar with the system, even a simple journey becomes a series of uncertain decisions.

Which ticket do I need?

Does it cover my entire journey?

Do I need to validate it?

What happens if I change trains?

Existing products are good at answering: "How do I get there?"

But there is another question underneath: "Am I doing this correctly?"

That confidence gap became the focus of EuroVoyage.

Opportunity

What if the traveler didn't have to learn the system?

Rather than giving users more information about how local transit works, I wanted to explore whether the product could make the decision for them.

How might we

Help travelers make the right transit decision for one journey without requiring them to understand the local transit system first?

This shifted the design goal from information delivery to decision support. The experience should answer three questions as quickly as possible:

01

What should I buy?

Recommend the appropriate ticket.

02

Why is this right?

Explain what it covers in plain language.

03

What do I do next?

Guide through validation and boarding.

Research

I wanted to understand where the uncertainty actually happened.

What I did

4 semi-structured interviews with travelers experienced with European transit

Competitive audit of Google Maps, Citymapper, Moovit, Rome2Rio

Secondary research into transit UX patterns across EU cities

Contextual observation notes from first-arrival scenarios

What I was looking for

Rather than asking whether people "liked" existing transit apps, I focused on the moments where they hesitated: where they stop and think, what information they struggle to interpret, what decisions they feel unsure about, and what happens after they purchase a ticket.

Core user needs

One clear ticket recommendation — no comparison, no zone math
Reassurance that the ticket is valid before and after purchase
Step-by-step guidance through purchase and boarding
One app — not three — to get from arrival to destination

Research limitation: the interview sample was small and exploratory. The purpose wasn't to statistically represent all travelers — it was to identify recurring behaviors and uncertainties that could be explored through prototyping and testing.

What I learned

The problem wasn't really route planning.

I spoke with 4 travelers — a first-time backpacker, a budget traveler, a frequent business traveler, and an anxious first-timer. Every single one had a story about getting it wrong. Not because they weren't trying, but because the systems gave them no real guidance at the moment they needed it.

P1 — F, 24, First-Time BackpackerPrague · First solo trip abroad · Relies on Google Maps at home
INT / 01
Q: Tell me about the last time you needed to use public transport in a new city.
"I landed in Prague and had no idea where to even buy a ticket. I downloaded an app, it was in Czech, I gave up and took a taxi. Way more expensive."
Q: What would have helped in that moment?
"Just tell me: here's your stop, here's the ticket, here's what to do. I don't need to understand the whole system. Just this one trip."
Key Insight

Users don't want to learn the system. They want one answer for one trip.

P2 — M, 31, Budget TravelerLisbon · Weekend trip · Travels 4–5 times/year across Europe
INT / 02
Q: How do you usually figure out which ticket to buy?
"I just pick something and hope it works. Usually I go for the cheapest option and then stress about whether I got the right one the entire ride."
Q: Have you ever been fined or had a problem?
"Once in Rome. I had a valid ticket but hadn't stamped it correctly. Cost me €50. I didn't even know stamping was a thing."
Key Insight

Buying the right ticket isn't the end — validation rules are equally invisible and equally punishing.

P3 — M, 38, Frequent Business TravelerAmsterdam · Conference trip · Travels 10+ times/year
INT / 03
Q: Do you use public transport when traveling for work?
"Rarely. I know I should but every city feels different and I don't have time to figure it out. I usually just take a cab and expense it."
Q: What would it take for you to actually use transit instead?
"If something just told me exactly what to do. One screen. I don't want to learn anything. Just get me to my meeting."
Key Insight

Even experienced travelers avoid transit when it feels complex. The barrier isn't capability — it's perceived effort.

P4 — F, 27, Anxious First-TimerVienna · Solo trip · Rarely travels, avoids transit when abroad
INT / 04
Q: How do you feel about using public transport in a city you've never been to?
"Honestly, I'm scared of getting it wrong. I'm worried I'll get fined, or board the wrong train, and then I'm stuck somewhere with my suitcase not knowing what to do."
Q: Even after you buy a ticket, does the anxiety go away?
"Not really. I keep second-guessing it. Like, is this the right one? Does it cover all the stops? I won't relax until I'm actually there."
Key Insight

Buying a ticket doesn't end the anxiety — it just shifts it. The design needed to close the confidence gap after purchase, not just before it.

Four consistent themes emerged

Across all research methods — interviews, competitive audit, and contextual observation — the same patterns appeared. Each cluster directly shaped a design decision.

The Insight

Users don't want to learn the transit system. They want confidence about one journey.

The solution wasn't to expose more fare information. It was to reduce the amount of information users had to interpret themselves.

Reduce decisions.
Increase confidence.

Every major product decision was evaluated against this principle. If something created unnecessary comparison, it could be removed. If something increased uncertainty, it needed clarification.

Competitive Landscape

Existing products solve the route. The opportunity was the decision.

I reviewed existing transit products to understand where they were already strong and where travelers still needed to figure things out on their own.

🗺️ Google Maps

Route Planning
Strength

Familiar route planning and directions with near-universal adoption and global coverage.

Opportunity

The experience does not primarily focus on simplifying local ticket decisions — no purchase, no zone guidance, no recommendation layer.

Gap

Excellent for routing. Silent on ticket selection — leaves the hardest decision to the user.

🏙️ Citymapper

Transit Specialist
Strength

Detailed transit planning with multi-modal journeys and smart alerts for disruptions.

Opportunity

Users may still need to understand local ticketing and fare rules; ticket purchasing not integrated in most cities.

Gap

Great for regular city users — not designed for the first-time traveler who doesn't know the system.

🚌 Moovit

Wide Coverage
Strength

Broad EU city coverage with step-by-step navigation and accessible route visualization.

Opportunity

Ticket selection and local fare understanding can still require additional interpretation; no single-recommendation model.

Gap

Useful mid-journey, but doesn't solve the pre-purchase decision problem.

🌍 Rome2Rio

Cross-City Planning
Strength

Multi-city and cross-country journey planning with price estimates across transport modes.

Opportunity

More focused on getting between destinations than onboarding a traveler into a local urban transit system.

Gap

Solves getting between cities — not getting around within them.

The gap

Existing products largely answer: "Where should I go?"

EuroVoyage focuses on: "What should I buy, why is it correct, and what do I do next?"

Design Strategy

Three decisions shaped EuroVoyage.

Rather than designing dozens of features, I focused the product around three decisions directly connected to the research.

01

Recommend instead of compare.

02

Make confidence part of the transaction.

03

The journey doesn't end at purchase.

📍

City Detection & Onboarding

Instead of asking users to configure the app or select their location manually, the experience detects the current city and immediately introduces the local transit system.

Why

Research showed users wanted to begin solving their journey immediately — not spend time setting up an app. Eliminating onboarding reduced initial friction and helped users transition directly from arrival to action.

🎟️

Smart Ticket Recommendation

Rather than presenting a list of unfamiliar ticket types, the interface recommends a single ticket supported by a short explanation.

"This ticket covers your entire journey, including transfers."
Why

Interview participants consistently described guessing when selecting tickets. The product makes a recommendation first — the user doesn't have to become a transit expert first.

🛡️

Confidence Reassurance Layer

Throughout the purchase flow, the interface reassures users that the selected ticket is valid, reducing uncertainty before it becomes hesitation. Instead of simply displaying a ticket name, the experience provides context about what it covers, which zones are included, and whether transfers are covered.

"Don't worry about the zones — we've handled that for you."
Why

Research showed that anxiety continued even after users purchased a ticket. The goal wasn't to add more information — it was to provide the specific information needed to trust the recommendation.

🚇

Step-by-Step Boarding Guide

After purchase, the experience shifts from transaction to navigation. Users receive guidance on which platform to use, how to validate the ticket, what to expect before boarding, and what to do next. The interface gradually surfaces only the information needed for the next action.

Why

Existing products often end support after payment. Interviews revealed that uncertainty actually peaks between buying the ticket and boarding, making this one of the most critical moments to support users.

🔁

Alternative Options (Collapsed)

Alternative ticket options remain available but hidden behind a collapsed section. Users who want to compare can expand it, while everyone else continues without interruption.

Why

Removing alternatives entirely limits flexibility. Showing every option by default recreates the same decision overload. Progressive disclosure balances simplicity with user control.

User Flow

One recommendation. One clear path.

The core journey was designed around reducing unnecessary choices.

The flow is deliberately short: enter destination → see recommended ticket → pay → get boarding guidance. It removes every optional step that doesn't directly help the user get on the right train.

At every stage, show the information needed for the next decision — not everything the system knows.

The Tradeoff

Simplicity vs. control.

Removing every alternative would make the experience simpler. But it would also remove user control. That wasn't the goal.

Show one recommendation by default and make alternatives available on demand.

Don't remove control. Remove unnecessary decisions.

The Moment

"So this definitely covers everything, right? Even if I change lines?"

The reassurance message was already visible. But the user still wasn't convinced.

The issue wasn't that the interface needed to reassure. It needed to show evidence.

Iteration

From reassurance to proof.

Reassurance → Evidence

Before

"Don't worry about the zones — we've handled that for you."

After

Valid for your planned journey

✓ Covers your route
✓ Includes required zones
✓ Transfers included

Trust isn't created by saying "don't worry." It's created by making the recommendation understandable.

CTA Copy — "Book Single Trip" → "Get This Ticket"

Users paused at "Book Single Trip" — unsure whether "single" meant one-way or a day pass. Changed to "Get This Ticket": action-clear, outcome-obvious.

"Show Other Options" — Moved Out of Focus

Even users who didn't want alternatives clicked out of curiosity, then felt confused. Reduced to low-visibility text below the primary CTA.

Confidence Gap at Boarding Unresolved — V2

One participant remained uncertain until they physically boarded. The reassurance worked at the decision point — but there was no persistent signal during boarding itself. A live validation indicator would address this.

What Testing Validated

The recommendation concept worked — partially.

Reducing choice helped users move forward. But testing also showed recommendation alone isn't enough.

A promising recommendation model with a remaining confidence problem.

Constraints & MVP

A good concept also needs to survive reality.

🔌

Transit API Fragmentation

Every European city runs its own transit API — some open, many proprietary.

💶

Ticketing Licensing

Selling tickets in-app requires agreements with each city's operator.

🔒

GDPR & Location

Auto-detection requires location permission with clear consent.

📵

Offline Reliability

Tickets and boarding info must work without connectivity.

Scoping the MVP

One city + one ticketing ecosystem + one clearly defined traveler scenario.

The product becomes more believable when the scope matches the complexity of the system.

What I would do next

  • Real-time disruption guidance
  • Persistent boarding confidence indicator
  • Offline-first city packs
  • Accessibility mode
Reflection

The biggest lesson wasn't about simplifying the interface. It was about simplifying the decision.

Research showed travelers didn't need more information — they needed confidence they were making the right choice. That led to EuroVoyage's core decision: recommend the right ticket and explain why.

Testing showed this approach reduces decision friction, but reassurance alone isn't enough. If I continued, I'd focus on carrying confidence from purchase all the way to boarding.

Reduce decisions.
Increase confidence.

The goal isn't to make travelers understand the transit system. The goal is to help them use it confidently.
