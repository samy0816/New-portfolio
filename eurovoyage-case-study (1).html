User Research

Understanding Travelers in Unfamiliar Cities

Research Methods

  • 4 semi-structured interviews with travelers familiar with European transit systems
  • Competitive audit of Google Maps, Citymapper, Moovit, Rome2Rio
  • Secondary research into transit UX patterns across EU cities
  • Contextual observation notes from first-arrival scenarios

Target Users

Travelers — tourists, students, and business visitors — arriving in a European city for the first time or infrequently, who need public transport but have no familiarity with the local ticketing system.

Key Research Observations

• Travelers struggled most during ticket selection rather than route planning
• Users relied on multiple apps to complete a single journey
• Ticket terminology, validation rules, and zone systems created uncertainty
• Even experienced travelers avoided transit when onboarding felt mentally demanding

Research Limitation: The interview sample size was exploratory and intended to identify behavioral patterns rather than statistically validate findings.

Core User Needs

✓ One clear ticket recommendation — no comparison, no zone math
✓ Reassurance that the ticket is valid before and after purchase
✓ Step-by-step guidance through purchase and boarding
✓ One app — not three — to get from arrival to destination

Competitor Analysis

Understanding the Current Landscape

🗺️ Google Maps

Competitor 1 — Most Used

✓ Strengths

  • Near-universal adoption and global coverage
  • Real-time transit data across most EU cities
  • Integrated directions from arrival point

✗ Weaknesses

  • No ticket purchase — routing only, not buying
  • Zone and pricing information absent or buried
  • No "which ticket do I need?" guidance layer
  • Multiple options presented without recommendation
Key Insight

Excellent for routing. Silent on ticket selection — leaves the hardest decision to the user.

🏙️ Citymapper

Competitor 2 — Transit Specialist

✓ Strengths

  • Deep transit data for major EU cities
  • Multi-modal journey planning (metro, bus, bike)
  • Smart alerts for disruptions and delays

✗ Weaknesses

  • City coverage limited — many EU cities not included
  • Ticket purchasing not integrated in most cities
  • Assumes the user already understands local zone logic
  • Onboarding does not address first-time city scenarios
Key Insight

Great for regular city users — not designed for the first-time traveler who doesn't know the system.

🚌 Moovit

Competitor 3 — Wide Coverage

✓ Strengths

  • Broad EU city coverage including smaller metros
  • Step-by-step navigation during journey
  • Accessible design and clear route visualisation

✗ Weaknesses

  • Ticket purchasing not integrated — redirects externally
  • No single-recommendation model — shows multiple routes without priority
  • Zone information not explained for new users
  • No reassurance layer for unfamiliar travelers
Key Insight

Useful mid-journey, but doesn't solve the pre-purchase decision problem.

🌍 Rome2Rio

Competitor 4 — Cross-City Planning

✓ Strengths

  • Multi-city and cross-country journey planning
  • Price estimates across transport modes
  • Wide transport mode coverage (bus, train, ferry)

✗ Weaknesses

  • Designed for inter-city travel — not local urban transit
  • Information density is high and can overwhelm first-time users
  • No ticket-buying — links out to third-party providers
  • No real-time data or step-by-step boarding guidance
Key Insight

Solves getting between cities — not getting around within them.

Key Product Opportunity

Existing transit apps optimize route planning, but none reduce uncertainty around ticket decisions and onboarding for first-time travelers.

User Interviews & Research Synthesis

4 Conversations, One Consistent Pattern

I spoke with 4 travelers — a first-time backpacker, a budget traveler, a frequent business traveler, and an anxious first-timer who avoids transit when abroad. Every single one had a story about getting it wrong. Not because they weren't trying, but because the systems gave them no real guidance at the moment they needed it.

P1 — F, 24, First-Time Backpacker Prague · First solo trip abroad · Relies on Google Maps at home
INT / 01
Q: Tell me about the last time you needed to use public transport in a new city.
"I landed in Prague and had no idea where to even buy a ticket. I downloaded an app, it was in Czech, I gave up and took a taxi. Way more expensive."
Q: What would have helped in that moment?
"Just tell me: here's your stop, here's the ticket, here's what to do. I don't need to understand the whole system. Just this one trip."
Key Insight

Users don't want to learn the system. They want one answer for one trip. Onboarding should be invisible — the first screen should already be about their journey, not about the app.

P2 — M, 31, Budget Traveler Lisbon · Weekend trip · Travels 4–5 times/year across Europe
INT / 02
Q: How do you usually figure out which ticket to buy?
"I just pick something and hope it works. Usually I go for the cheapest option and then stress about whether I got the right one the entire ride."
Q: Have you ever been fined or had a problem?
"Once in Rome. I had a valid ticket but hadn't stamped it correctly. Cost me €50. I didn't even know stamping was a thing."
Key Insight

Buying the right ticket isn't the end of the journey — validation rules are equally invisible and equally punishing. The app needs to cover "what to do after you buy" just as much as the purchase itself.

P3 — M, 38, Frequent Business Traveler Amsterdam · Conference trip · Travels 10+ times/year
INT / 03
Q: Do you use public transport when traveling for work?
"Rarely. I know I should but every city feels different and I don't have time to figure it out. I usually just take a cab and expense it."
Q: What would it take for you to actually use transit instead?
"If something just told me exactly what to do. One screen. I don't want to learn anything. Just get me to my meeting."
Key Insight

Even experienced travelers avoid transit when it feels complex. The barrier isn't capability — it's perceived effort. A solution that feels effortless will convert users who've already given up on transit.

P4 — F, 27, Anxious First-Timer Vienna · Solo trip · Rarely travels, avoids transit when abroad
INT / 04
Q: How do you feel about using public transport in a city you've never been to?
"Honestly, I'm scared of getting it wrong. I'm worried I'll get fined, or board the wrong train, and then I'm stuck somewhere with my suitcase not knowing what to do."
Q: Even after you buy a ticket, does the anxiety go away?
"Not really. I keep second-guessing it. Like, is this the right one? Does it cover all the stops? I won't relax until I'm actually there."
Key Insight

Buying a ticket doesn't end the anxiety — it just shifts it. The design needed to close the confidence gap after purchase, not just before it. This interview directly shaped the post-purchase reassurance screen.

Core Product Decisions

Screens scoped to the four research clusters: decision overload, confidence gap, app fragmentation, and zone opacity.

📍

City Detection & Onboarding

Auto-detects the user's current city on first open. Introduces the local transit system in one sentence and confirms it's ready to help — no setup required.

🎟️

Smart Ticket Recommendation

Single recommended ticket, clearly labelled, with one-line reasoning ("This covers your entire route and any transfers"). No comparison grid, no zone math.

🛡️

Confidence Reassurance Layer

Persistent messaging at each step confirming the ticket is valid: "Don't worry about the zones, we've handled the math." Reduces hesitation at purchase and at boarding.

🚇

Step-by-Step Boarding Guide

After ticket selection: which platform, what to look for, how to validate. Real-time guidance through the transit system, not just to it.

🔁

Alternative Options (Collapsed)

Other ticket options available — but collapsed by default. Users who want to compare can expand; users who want to act don't need to engage with it at all.

User Journey Map

From Arrival to Confident Boarding

The journey map revealed that the highest-friction moment occurs between arriving at the station and boarding — where uncertainty and cognitive load are highest.

User Flow

Core Task: Get to Destination via Public Transport

The flow is deliberately short: enter destination → see recommended ticket → pay → get boarding guidance. It removes every optional step that doesn't directly help the user get on the right train. No account creation, no zone selection, no mode comparison. The design forces a recommendation, not a choice.

Usability Testing & Results

I conducted quick usability tests with 8 participants who had experience traveling in unfamiliar European cities. Each was given a single task to complete without guidance or coaching.

Primary Scenario:

"You've just landed in a new city. Use this app to get to your destination using public transport."

5/8

Trusted the recommended route immediately.
Users followed the single recommendation flow without comparing multiple options.

4/8

Reassurance messaging reduced hesitation.
Users responded positively to confidence-building copy during ticket selection.

6/8

Users hesitated before payment confirmation.
Additional reassurance was needed before final purchase.

3/8

Alternative options distracted some users.
Secondary actions competed with the primary recommendation flow.

User Quotes

💬

"I didn't even think about zones. It just told me what to get and I trusted it. That's exactly what I want when I land somewhere new."

💬

"The button said 'Book Single Trip' — I wasn't sure if single trip meant one way or return. I'd want something like 'Get This Ticket' or just 'Continue'."

This directly triggered the CTA copy iteration — changed from "Book Single Trip" to a more action-oriented label in the revised design.

💬

"So this definitely covers everything, right? Even if I change lines?" — asked out loud, even after the reassurance copy was visible. The confidence gap is real even with messaging present.

Improvements After Testing

Based on User Feedback — Iterations Made

CTA Copy — "Book Single Trip" → "Get This Ticket"

Users paused at "Book Single Trip" — unsure whether "single" meant one-way or a single-day pass. Changed to "Get This Ticket": action-clear, outcome-obvious, no ambiguity. Users completed the purchase step with less hesitation after the CTA update.

Stronger Confirmation After Purchase

The reassurance copy on the ticket screen was present but passive. Users still asked "does this definitely cover everything?" out loud. Added a dedicated post-purchase confirmation screen with a clear validity statement and the three boarding steps. Informing isn't the same as reassuring.

"Show Other Options" — Moved Out of Focus

Originally a visible link next to the recommendation. Even users who didn't want alternatives clicked it out of curiosity, then felt more confused. Reduced to low-visibility text below the primary CTA. Still there for users who need it — just not competing for attention with the main action.

Confidence Gap at Boarding Unresolved — V2

Even after purchase, one participant remained uncertain whether their ticket was valid for the full journey until they physically boarded and nothing happened. The app's reassurance copy worked at the decision point — but there was no persistent "you're good" signal during the boarding moment itself. A live validation indicator or push notification at the moment of entry would address this, but wasn't implemented within the project timeline. Documented here rather than hidden because it's exactly the kind of gap that matters in production.

Realism & Constraints

🔌

Transit API Fragmentation

Every European city runs its own transit API — some open, many proprietary. Real implementation requires provider partnerships or a third-party aggregator like Navitia. No unified European standard exists yet.

💶

Ticketing Licensing

Selling transit tickets in-app requires agreements with each city's operator. Not all cities offer open ticketing access — some require operator-specific SDKs, NFC hardware, or local regulatory approval.

🔒

GDPR & Location Consent

Auto-detecting the user's city requires location permission. GDPR mandates clear consent and data minimisation — location data should not persist beyond the active session and must be handled transparently.

📵

Offline Reliability

Travelers at airports often have limited data connectivity. Purchased tickets and boarding instructions must work fully offline — otherwise the app fails at exactly the moment it's most needed.

Future Expansion

  • Real-time disruption alerts: platform changes, delays, strike notices surfaced in plain language
  • Persistent boarding confidence indicator — the unresolved V2 item flagged during testing
  • Offline-first city packs — downloadable before arrival for zero-connectivity scenarios
  • Accessibility mode: larger targets, higher contrast, VoiceOver support for users with visual impairments

Product Impact

  • Reduced time-to-ticket purchase
  • Higher confidence during ticket selection and boarding
  • Lower cognitive load during transit onboarding
  • Improved willingness to use public transit in unfamiliar cities

Reflection

This project reinforced how strongly uncertainty affects user behavior during high-stress travel moments. While existing tools already provide route information, users still lacked confidence in ticket selection and boarding decisions.

The most effective design decision was reducing unnecessary choice through a single recommended flow paired with reassurance messaging. Testing showed that simplifying decisions increased trust — but only when users clearly understood why the recommendation was valid.

If I continued the project, I would focus next on the boarding experience itself, where anxiety still persisted even after ticket purchase.
