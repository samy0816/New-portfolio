EuroVoyage — a decision-first transit experience

The problem isn't finding the train. It's knowing you're doing it right. A decision-first transit experience for travelers navigating unfamiliar European cities.

Problem

Finding a route is easy. Knowing how to use the transit system is not.

When travelers arrive in an unfamiliar city, they often already know where they want to go. What they don't know is how the local transit system works.

Different cities use different ticket types, fare zones, validation rules, terminology, payment systems, and transfer rules. For someone unfamiliar with the system, even a simple journey becomes a series of uncertain decisions.

Which ticket do I need?

Does it cover my entire journey?

Do I need to validate it?

What happens if I change trains?

Existing products are good at answering: "How do I get there?"

But there is another question underneath: "Am I doing this correctly?"

That confidence gap became the focus of EuroVoyage.

Opportunity

What if the traveler didn't have to learn the system?

Rather than giving users more information about how local transit works, I wanted to explore whether the product could make the decision for them.

How might we

Help travelers make the right transit decision for one journey without requiring them to understand the local transit system first?

This shifted the design goal from information delivery to decision support. The experience should answer three questions as quickly as possible:

01

What should I buy?

Recommend the appropriate ticket.

02

Why is this right?

Explain what it covers in plain language.

03

What do I do next?

Guide through validation and boarding.

Research

I wanted to understand where the uncertainty actually happened.

What I did

4 semi-structured interviews with travelers experienced with European transit

Competitive audit of Google Maps, Citymapper, Moovit, Rome2Rio

Secondary research into transit UX patterns across EU cities

Contextual observation notes from first-arrival scenarios

The research lens

Rather than asking whether people "liked" existing transit apps, I mapped the moments where confidence broke down: what travelers struggled to interpret, which decisions they second-guessed, and whether buying a ticket actually resolved their uncertainty.

Core user needs

One clear ticket recommendation — no comparison, no zone math
Reassurance that the ticket is valid before and after purchase
Step-by-step guidance through purchase and boarding
One app — not three — to get from arrival to destination

Research limitation: the interview sample was small and exploratory. The purpose wasn't to statistically represent all travelers — it was to identify recurring behaviors and uncertainties that could be explored through prototyping and testing.

What I learned

The same uncertainty appeared in different forms.

I spoke with 4 travelers with different levels of experience, travel frequency, and confidence. The situations varied, but the moments of uncertainty were remarkably consistent: choosing a ticket, understanding what it covered, and knowing what to do once the journey had started.

The problem was not simply a lack of transit information. It was the burden of interpreting that information correctly under time pressure.

From observations to design direction

I clustered the observations around recurring moments of uncertainty. The synthesis revealed four patterns — not isolated complaints — that repeatedly shaped how travelers made decisions and where they lost confidence.

Research synthesis

To make the path from evidence to design easier to follow, I translated the recurring observations into the product implications they created.

Observation

Travelers did not want to learn an entire transit system for one journey.

Pattern

The burden came from interpreting unfamiliar rules, not simply finding information.

Design implication

Recommend one appropriate option first instead of asking users to compare the system themselves.

Observation

Buying a ticket did not eliminate uncertainty when coverage and validation rules remained unclear.

Pattern

Confidence continued after the transaction rather than ending at purchase.

Design implication

Show evidence about validity and continue guiding the traveler through the next action.

Observation

Experienced travelers still avoided transit when figuring it out felt like too much effort.

Pattern

Perceived cognitive effort could outweigh the cost benefit of public transport.

Design implication

Prioritize speed and low cognitive effort alongside clarity and reassurance.

Observation

Some travelers continued second-guessing their decisions even after choosing or buying a ticket.

Pattern

A correct decision was not enough if users could not understand why it was correct.

Design implication

Make recommendations inspectable by exposing the facts users need to verify them.

Recommend one clear option, explain what it covers, and make the next action explicit.

Daniel

Daniel travels frequently for work and values speed, clarity, and low-effort navigation when arriving in unfamiliar cities.

Pain Points

  • Every city feels different — can't build habits across trips
  • No time to research — figures it out at the machine, stressed
  • Has taken taxis to avoid the confusion, despite costing 4× more
  • Feels embarrassed asking locals for help with a basic task

Values

  • Speed — needs to be somewhere, now
  • Reliability — can't afford to board the wrong train
  • Low effort — one less thing to think about

"Every city feels different. I never know if what worked in Vienna will work in Amsterdam."

Design implication

Keep the primary journey path fast and low-effort, with a consistent interaction model across cities.

Shared Job To Be Done

One core job, two different thresholds for effort.

Job To Be Done

“When I arrive in an unfamiliar city and need to reach my destination, help me make the right transit decision and guide me through the journey—without requiring me to learn the local system first.”

Maya needs clarity and confidence to overcome anxiety. Daniel needs speed and minimal cognitive effort. The product needed to support both without creating separate experiences.

The Insight

Users don't want to learn the transit system. They want confidence about one journey.

The solution wasn't to expose more fare information. It was to reduce the amount of information users had to interpret themselves.

Reduce decisions.
Increase confidence.

Every major product decision was evaluated against this principle. If something created unnecessary comparison, it could be removed. If something increased uncertainty, it needed clarification.

The gap

Existing products largely answer: "Where should I go?"

EuroVoyage focuses on: "What should I buy, why is it correct, and what do I do next?"

Design Strategy

Three decisions shaped EuroVoyage.

Rather than designing dozens of features, I focused the product around three decisions directly connected to the research.

01

Recommend instead of compare.

02

Make confidence part of the transaction.

03

The journey doesn't end at purchase.

📍

City Detection & Onboarding

Instead of asking users to configure the app or select their location manually, the experience detects the current city and immediately introduces the local transit system.

Why

Research showed users wanted to begin solving their journey immediately — not spend time setting up an app. Eliminating onboarding reduced initial friction and helped users transition directly from arrival to action.

🎟️

Smart Ticket Recommendation

Rather than presenting a list of unfamiliar ticket types, the interface recommends a single ticket supported by a short explanation.

"This ticket covers your entire journey, including transfers."
Why

Interview participants consistently described guessing when selecting tickets. The product makes a recommendation first — the user doesn't have to become a transit expert first.

🛡️

Make the Recommendation Inspectable

A recommendation should reduce cognitive effort without becoming a black box. Instead of simply displaying a ticket name, the experience shows the specific facts a traveler needs to verify: where the ticket is valid, which zones or route it covers, whether transfers are included, and what action is required next.

"Valid for your planned journey — route covered, required zones included, transfers supported."
Research signal

Travelers did not simply want to be told that a ticket was correct. They wanted enough evidence to stop second-guessing the decision.

🚇

Step-by-Step Boarding Guide

After purchase, the experience shifts from transaction to navigation. Users receive guidance on which platform to use, how to validate the ticket, what to expect before boarding, and what to do next. The interface gradually surfaces only the information needed for the next action.

Why

Existing products often end support after payment. Interviews revealed that uncertainty actually peaks between buying the ticket and boarding, making this one of the most critical moments to support users.

🔁

Alternative Options (Collapsed)

Alternative ticket options remain available but hidden behind a collapsed section. Users who want to compare can expand it, while everyone else continues without interruption.

Why

Removing alternatives entirely limits flexibility. Showing every option by default recreates the same decision overload. Progressive disclosure balances simplicity with user control.

User Flow

One recommendation. One clear path.

The core journey was designed around reducing unnecessary choices.

The flow is deliberately short: enter destination → see recommended ticket → pay → get boarding guidance. It removes every optional step that doesn't directly help the user get on the right train.

At every stage, show the information needed for the next decision — not everything the system knows.

The Tradeoff

Simplicity vs. control.

Removing every alternative would make the experience simpler. But it would also remove user control. That wasn't the goal.

Show one recommendation by default and make alternatives available on demand.

Don't remove control. Remove unnecessary decisions.

CTA Copy — "Book Single Trip" → "Get This Ticket"

Users paused at "Book Single Trip" — unsure whether "single" meant one-way or a day pass. Changed to "Get This Ticket": action-clear, outcome-obvious.

"Show Other Options" — Moved Out of Focus

Even users who didn't want alternatives clicked out of curiosity, then felt confused. Reduced to low-visibility text below the primary CTA.

Confidence Gap at Boarding Unresolved — V2 hypothesis

One participant remained uncertain until they physically boarded. The reassurance worked at the decision point — but there was no persistent signal during boarding itself. A live validation indicator was proposed for V2; it was not formally retested within this project.

The revised version was not formally retested within the project timeline. Validating whether evidence-based messaging reduces hesitation would be the first priority for V2.

What Testing Validated

The recommendation concept worked — partially.

The recommendation reduced decision overload for many participants, but payment hesitation showed that the design still needed to make its reasoning visible.

The direction was promising. The next question was whether evidence—not reassurance—could close the remaining hesitation.

From Concept to Product

The experience is multi-city by design. The infrastructure is not yet solved.

EuroVoyage was intentionally designed around travel across unfamiliar European cities. The inconsistency between transit systems is the core problem — not something I wanted to remove from the scenario to make the concept easier to build.

This case study therefore explores the experience model: how one consistent, decision-first interaction could help travelers navigate different local systems. Turning that concept into a real product would introduce a separate layer of product and operational complexity.

🔌

Transit Data Normalization

Routes, fares, zones, and service information are exposed differently across operators. The product would need a reliable way to translate those systems into one consistent decision model.

💶

Ticketing & Operator Partnerships

Recommending a ticket and selling a ticket are different capabilities. In-app purchasing would depend on commercial and technical relationships with local operators.

🔒

Location, Consent & Trust

Automatic city detection should reduce setup effort without making users feel monitored. Location access would need clear consent and a useful fallback when permission is unavailable.

📵

Reliability Without Connectivity

A traveler cannot lose access to a valid ticket or the next required action simply because connectivity drops underground or while roaming.

A phased launch would validate the infrastructure — not shrink the problem.

The product vision remains multi-city. A real launch would need to prove the underlying model incrementally: integrate a limited number of transit ecosystems, validate whether fare and ticket rules can be normalized reliably, then expand coverage without compromising recommendation accuracy.

What I would validate next

  • Confidence during disruption — How does the experience preserve trust when the recommended journey changes unexpectedly?
  • Recommendation accuracy across cities — Can different fare structures and validation rules be translated into one consistent recommendation model?
  • Confidence after the decision — Can a persistent journey state reduce second-guessing between ticket purchase and boarding?
  • Failure states — What should happen when location, connectivity, real-time data, or ticketing integrations are unavailable?
Reflection

The hardest part wasn't simplifying the interface. It was deciding what the traveler shouldn't have to think about.

Research shifted the problem from making transit systems easier to understand to helping travelers make one correct decision with enough confidence to continue.

That distinction shaped the product: recommend rather than overwhelm, explain rather than simply reassure, and continue supporting the traveler after the transaction. Reducing complexity does not mean hiding it—it means removing what users should not have to manage while exposing the evidence they need to trust the decision.

Reduce decisions.
Preserve understanding.
Increase confidence.

The goal isn't to make travelers understand the entire transit system. It's to help them understand enough to act with confidence.
