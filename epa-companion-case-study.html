Project Overview

The Challenge

Problem Statement

Germany rolled out electronic patient records (ePA) for statutory insurance members in 2025. By early 2026, awareness of the system had grown significantly, but active usage remained limited. For many patients, the experience still felt fragmented: medical information was often presented as disconnected PDFs and technical terminology with little contextual guidance.

The ePA was technically live. Experientially, it was dead.

+
The Vision

Goal

Design a mobile app that helps people make sense of documents already in their ePA — without needing medical literacy or German fluency. Research showed the barrier wasn't data access, it was interpretation. Every major design decision traces directly to one of four research-defined clusters: comprehension, trust, language, and passivity.

User Research

Understanding ePA Users Across Germany

Research Methods

  • 5 semi-structured interviews (German nationals + international residents)
  • Competitive audit of TK App, AOK App, Ada Health, Vivy
  • Secondary research into GEMEINSAM DIGITAL 2026 strategy — which revealed the government's own adoption targets were being missed due to UX barriers, not technical ones
  • EU AI Act health provisions review — used as a design constraint, not a post-hoc compliance check

Target Users

Germans and international residents with statutory health insurance who have ePA access but cannot meaningfully understand, act on, or trust the health data stored in it.

Competitor Analysis

Understanding the Current Landscape

🥇 TK App (Techniker Krankenkasse)

Competitor 1 — Most Used

✓ Strengths

  • One of Germany's largest insurers (~11M+ members)
  • Official ePA integration (TK-Safe)
  • High trust due to institutional backing

✗ Weaknesses

  • ePA features are not prominently surfaced (multi-step navigation)
  • Medical documents presented with low interpretability (file-based view)
  • No intelligent explanation layer (no AI or guided insights)
  • Primarily German-language interface, limiting accessibility
Key Insight

Strong on infrastructure, weak on usability + comprehension

🥈 AOK App

Competitor 2

✓ Strengths

  • Largest public insurer group in Germany (tens of millions covered)
  • Broad service ecosystem (appointments, claims, records)
  • ePA access integrated across regional apps

✗ Weaknesses

  • Functions largely as a passive document repository
  • Limited guidance or interpretation of medical data
  • Sharing/access workflows can be fragmented or non-intuitive
  • UX perceived as functional but outdated (based on user reviews)
Key Insight

High reach, but low engagement due to passive experience

🥉 Ada Health

Competitor 3 — AI Leader

✓ Strengths

  • Globally recognized AI symptom checker
  • Strong trust framing and clinical credibility
  • Multilingual, consumer-friendly UX
  • High engagement due to interactive flow

✗ Weaknesses

  • No native ePA integration
  • Does not analyze or interpret existing medical documents
  • Focused on new assessments, not longitudinal records
Key Insight

Excellent at answering "What could this be?" — not designed for "What does my record mean?"

⚠️ Vivy (Discontinued 2023)

Competitor 4 — Reference Case

✓ Strengths

  • Early leader in centralized health record access
  • Clean, modern UX compared to insurer apps
  • Multi-insurer collaboration model

✗ Weaknesses

  • Struggled with user retention and sustained engagement
  • Value proposition unclear beyond document storage
  • Increasing overlap with insurer-led ePA solutions
  • No strong interpretation or AI-driven insights layer
Key Insight

Solved aggregation, but not user motivation or understanding

The Gap

TK had the data. Ada had the AI UX. Neither had both — and neither treated document comprehension as the primary use case. That's the gap EpaBuddy is designed to close. The opportunity isn't technical — the insurance infrastructure already exists. What's missing is an interpretation layer that sits between the raw record and the confused user, and treats understanding as the core product, not an afterthought. This informed the entire design direction: every feature decision traces back to one of the four research clusters.

User Interviews

Five Conversations, One Consistent Truth

I spoke with 5 participants across Germany — an international student from India, a German software engineer, a nurse with clinical context, a Syrian undergraduate, and a German marketing manager. Every participant had tried their health insurance app. Every participant had stopped using it.

P1 — F, 24, Exchange Student from India Munich · TK Insurance · A2 German
INT / 01
Q: Have you ever opened your health insurance app to look at your records?
"Yes once. I saw some files but they were all in German and I had no idea what they were. I just closed it."
Q: What would make you not trust an AI health explanation?
"If it sounded too confident. If it said 'you have this condition' I would be scared. But if it says 'this number is slightly low, worth asking your doctor' that feels okay."
Key Insight

Users want hedged, conversational AI tone — not diagnostic, not robotic. The framing of certainty directly shapes emotional response.

P2 — M, 31, Software Engineer (German) Berlin · AOK Insurance · Native German
INT / 02
Q: When did you last open your ePA?
"Maybe six months ago. There was a PDF from a specialist. I couldn't tell if it was a diagnosis or just notes from the visit."
Q: Would you want AI to summarize your documents?
"Yes but I'd want to know it's AI. I don't want to think I'm reading a doctor's summary and then find out it was generated."
Key Insight

Transparency about AI authorship is non-negotiable. Users don't resist AI — they resist AI that conceals itself.

P3 — F, 29, Nurse Hamburg · Clinical context · Native German
INT / 03
Q: What do your patients struggle with around digital health records?
"They get a Befund after a specialist visit and have no idea what it says. If they had something that explained it simply, we'd get fewer anxious calls."
Q: Any concerns about AI explaining health data?
"The explanation has to be clearly not a diagnosis. If an AI says 'your iron is low' some patients immediately think cancer. Framing matters enormously."
Key Insight

The "Not medical advice" label isn't legal cover — it actively manages user emotional response. Design it as a first-class UI element, not a footnote.

P4 — M, 22, Undergraduate Student from Syria Saarbrücken · AOK Insurance · B1 German
INT / 04
Q: Have you ever needed to share health records with a new doctor?
"Yes when I changed GP. They asked for my previous records. I didn't know how to get them so I just said I don't have them."
Q: What language do you want health information in?
"Arabic or English. My German isn't good enough for medical stuff."
Key Insight

Language barriers cause users to abandon health interactions entirely. Multilingual design isn't a feature addition — it's accessibility.

P5 — F, 38, Marketing Manager (German) Frankfurt · TK Insurance · Health-conscious
INT / 05
Q: Have you ever looked at a lab result in the app?
"I tried. There was a table of numbers and some were flagged in red. I didn't know if red was serious or just a formatting thing. I closed it."
Q: What would a good experience look like?
"Like how banking apps say 'you spent more than usual this month.' Just tell me what I need to know."
Key Insight

Users want proactive surfacing of relevant information, not passive storage. The mental model is a smart assistant, not a filing cabinet.

A Note on Persona Scope

Asel and Jonas represent motivated users who want to understand their health data but can't. A third pattern emerged during interviews — the actively resistant user — but the project scope didn't support a third persona. Instead, their perspectives (discomfort with invisible AI, concern about patient anxiety from plain-language summaries) directly shaped the trust layer: the transparency badge, the four confidence states, and the placement of the "Not medical advice" label all exist to design for the skeptic, not just the willing user.

Feature Set

Each feature maps directly to a research cluster — comprehension, trust, language, or engagement.

3-step explainer answering the question Vivy never did: "Why should I give you access?" Insurance connection flow designed to earn trust before asking for data.

📋

Health Timeline

Chronological living feed of all health events. Proactive AI nudge cards. Filter by type. The ePA as a narrative, not a file drawer.

AI Document Reader

Plain-language AI summary with transparency badge, confidence indicator, and "Not medical advice" label. Four designed states: loading, success, low confidence, fallback.

Visual range indicators per result. Tap any value for AI contextual explanation. Separate from document reader — structured data deserves structured UI treatment.

📅

🔒

Consent & Sharing

Who has access to what, with per-provider toggle controls. GDPR-respectful, transparent, genuinely simple. No phone calls required.

💊

Medication View

Active and past prescriptions. AI plain-language explanation of each medication's purpose and common side effects. Refill reminder toggles.

🌐

Language & Settings

Language selector (DE / EN / TR / AR), AI explanation preferences, per-event notification controls. Language is a core feature, not a preference.

✦

Empty State

The screen most designers forget. Designed to reassure, explain, and convert — not just display a "no data" message.

Major Screens

Onboarding — Trust Setup

Trust-First Entry Point

A 3-step explainer that answers the question Vivy never answered: "Why should I give you access to my data?" The interface explains what the ePA is, what the AI does, and critically — what it does not do — before asking for any permissions.

Design Thought

Two participants declined to connect health apps to their insurance data because they didn't understand what the app would do with it. That grounded the decision to replace a standard permission prompt with a 3-step explainer. Each step answers a specific interview question: what the ePA is, what the AI does with the data, and what it cannot do.

The copy went through 3 revisions. Biggest change: removing "AI-powered" — which caused participants to skim — and replacing it with the plain-language benefit. An earlier screen 3 headline, "Limitations of AI," made two participants less confident before they'd used the product. It became "What the AI doesn't do" — same content, framing that acknowledges limits without leading with them.

AI Document Reader — The Centrepiece

The most important screen in the product. Instead of a raw PDF, the user sees a plain-language AI summary with a transparency badge, confidence indicator, and a clearly visible "Not medical advice" label. Four designed states ensure the experience never breaks — even when AI can't fully process a document.

Design Thought

In early wireframes, "Not medical advice" sat at the bottom as a small footer — the default pattern for health app disclaimers. P3 (nurse) was explicit: patients emotionally anchor on the AI interpretation before reading anything below it. That moved the label to immediately below the summary header, at the same visual weight as the content itself.

The confidence indicator was originally colour-only. Two usability participants didn't notice it; a third found the red state "scary" without reading the label. Colour alone was doing too much emotional work. Adding an icon and short text label — "High confidence · Based on structured data" — made it both visible and legible. The cleaner version was the wrong version. This design also aligns with EU AI Act Article 13: disclosure at point of use, not buried in settings.

Pre-Visit Prep

Never Walk In Unprepared Again

Before a doctor appointment, the AI surfaces the 2–3 most relevant records for that visit type and suggests questions to consider asking based on recent health data. One-tap sharing sends selected records to the upcoming provider.

Design Thought

Jonas's JTBD was "walk in prepared, not starting from zero." After testing, I reduced the default record list from "all relevant records" to the AI-curated 2–3 most relevant — because 3 users felt overwhelmed by the full list and didn't know where to start.

Improvements After Testing

Based on User Feedback — 2 Rounds of Iteration< (Changes are already present in above hifi protoype section)

AI Confidence Indicator Redesign

Originally colour-only (green/amber/red). 2 of 5 users didn't notice it at all. Redesigned as colour + icon + short text label ("High confidence · Based on structured data"). Now impossible to miss and actually communicates meaning.

"Not Medical Advice" Label Position

Originally appeared only at the bottom of the summary — users scrolled past it without reading. Moved to immediately below the AI summary header, the first thing a user sees after the explanation title. Always in view, always first.

Pre-Visit Record Curation

Originally showed all relevant records by default. 3 users felt overwhelmed and didn't know where to start. AI now surfaces only the 2–3 most relevant records for the specific appointment type. Full list available via "See all records."

Confidence Badge Interaction Unresolved — V2

During testing, one participant tapped the confidence badge expecting it to expand or explain itself — and nothing happened. The badge was display-only. This is flagged as an unresolved issue: a tooltip or expandable explanation on the confidence indicator is the right behaviour but wasn't implemented within the project timeline. It's documented here rather than hidden because it's the kind of detail that matters in production.

Realism & Constraints

🔌

Insurance API Access Required

Requires official TK/AOK ePA API integration and GesundheitsID authentication for real patient data access

⚖️

EU AI Act Compliance

Health AI is classified high-risk under the EU AI Act — all transparency, confidence, and override design decisions were made with this in mind

🔒

DSGVO / GDPR

Must ensure end-to-end encryption, minimal data collection, and explicit per-provider consent for all health data shared through the app

🏥

Medical Accuracy

AI copy would require clinical review before production. This shaped the design choice to use hedged language ("slightly below normal" vs "low") and four confidence states — so the UI communicates certainty level even before copy is validated.

Reflection

The biggest surprise: AI tone mattered more than AI accuracy. Participants didn't verify whether summaries were medically correct — they trusted or distrusted them based on how the language made them feel. Hedged, conversational phrasing built more trust than precise clinical language. That's a UX finding with real implications for how health AI should be written, and it's not something I expected going in.

The second lesson: I cut a feature. An early version included a symptom-checking flow — a natural extension since users were already in the app reading records. After testing, I removed it. Users already had Ada for that. Adding it would have muddied the product's identity without meaningful value. Knowing what to cut is harder than knowing what to build. This was my one clean example of making that call without hedging it.
